# Landing page & conversion playbook

The single most-repeated body of judgment across the sources. Use it when building or reviewing any marketing page, homepage, product page, pricing page, or signup flow. It is deliberately opinionated; deviate when you can say why.

## The four questions, in order

A stranger arriving on the page resolves these in sequence, and a miss higher up cannot be fixed lower down:

1. **What is it?** — one plain headline; if the headline is clever, add a literal sub-headline.
2. **Is it for me?** — name the audience and the segment (developers, US C-corps, restaurant owners, construction ops). Persona pages convert better than a generic home.
3. **Does it work / can I trust it?** — show the product; proof from real people with names, companies, faces; press; counts; a live demo.
4. **What do I do next?** — one primary action, unmistakable.

Answer 1–2 above the fold. 3 begins above the fold (product shot / demo) and continues below. 4 is repeated.

## First-impression discipline

- Five-second test: show the hero, hide it, ask what it does and who it's for. If the answer is "purple" or "something with cleaning", rewrite.
- One headline, one byline, one CTA. Two equal-weight columns tell the reader both matter, so neither wins.
- The real headline is often one scroll below — promote it.
- Headline-skim test: read only the headings down the page. The story must survive. Headings are claims, not labels ("Features", "Why us").
- Don't make me think: no coined nouns without explanation ("Artisans", "Strata"), no invented concepts nobody would search ("progressive discovery"). Use the buyer's language — ask customers "how would you describe us?" and use their phrase.
- Value over features: what the person gets ("rented faster, more revenue") before what the software does. Turn features into pains solved.
- Say the differentiator out loud. If a mechanic makes you different (group buying, hosted open-source, speed), it belongs at the top, not buried.
- Prefer the positive statement to a negative one ("stop following tutorials"): skimmers read only the bold, so don't bold the negatives.
- Specific over vague: "event management software for live events and webinars" beats "go beyond live events". Concrete claims (30% cheaper) resonate; overclaims ("most admired companies in the world") don't.
- Two names for one product, or describing yourself only as "X for Y + Z" — pick one name, own your own description.

## Show the product

- Real product beats metaphor. Zoom to the meat; blur or crop the rest; slow down and overlay text at key beats. A far-away full screenshot is unreadable to a stranger.
- A video thumbnail must be informative even if never played. Auto-advancing carousels and tabs are unseen by most; assume nothing behind a tab is read; if you must, tie tabs to scroll.
- For AI products: a "feat of strength" — type a little, get a magic result — on the page, before any account. Provide example prompts as one-click buttons; constrain the first demo so it succeeds; guide demo agents toward the buyer's problem instead of "what do you want to talk about?".
- Let people try, then gate on export/save/share, not on entry. A "try it" that hits a wall (or a hand-cursor that does nothing) is a broken promise.
- Dev tools: repo link with stars, playground, syntax-highlighted real code at least as large as body copy, hand-wrapped, annotated line by line; pricing signals a product, not a project.

## CTA discipline

- One primary CTA per screen; secondary actions are links, not equal buttons.
- Same text and color repeated down the page and in the footer; sticky on long pages; animate it in when the reader reaches the decision point.
- The button says what happens ("Start free", "Watch the 2-minute walkthrough"). "Book a demo" is a dramatic first ask — convince first, or offer the lighter step. Embed the email field instead of hiding it behind a button. Handle the objection on the button ("no credit card").
- Same widget must behave the same everywhere (a toggle-looking login/demo pair next to a real toggle is confusion).
- Don't ask for money before explaining. Don't put your accelerator badge where your logo should be.

## Proof and trust

- Faces + names + companies + press; a founder story/about page (your own, not a launch-post link); live chat that a founder actually answers; a case study with a known logo; counts in high-stakes domains.
- Testimonials that lack company names or read as Written In Title Case feel fake and taint the numbers next to them.
- Trust-sensitive domains (money, health, legal, identity): the fit-and-finish bar is much higher; anything that reads "vibe-coded" or "hack project" costs conversions.
- Be authentic to stage: a very polished site on a month-old product sets the wrong expectation; own being small.

## Motion

- Motion is a spotlight, not weather. Use it on the one thing you want read first, or to make a point overly obvious. Everything else: still.
- Sins: multiple simultaneous animations, wrapping sentences in motion, sideways or fast tickers, moving grids behind text, glows on buttons, scroll-hijacking, "scroll to explore", decorative preloaders, animating the CTA before people can read.
- Calm reads as established. If it's your first animation, get someone experienced to look at it.

## Structure and length

- Pyramid: one clear thing at the top, expand as you scroll; deeper pages for features, security, customer stories, pricing. Multiple pages are allowed; the nav is a place for targeted pages ("for coaches").
- People scroll more than they click, and they scroll a lot. Don't cram everything above the fold; do put the biggest clue (the industry footage, the how-it-works) high enough.
- Enterprise pages are not conversion machines: buyers need materials in deep pages you link from email; keep a way to try anyway.
- Homepage as front page: enough to decide it's interesting, not so much you're overwhelmed.

## Fit and finish (the "what else don't they care about?" list)

Line-height and section gaps, alignment, contrast (no light gray on dark, no white on brand-yellow), max-width on text, consistent casing, working hovers and links, no double spaces or spaces before periods, consistent radii/shadows/borders (no boxes in boxes), tap targets, no obvious bugs above the fold, mobile and tablet checked, fast load.

## Funnel work

Map every step from arrival to the aha (the moment value is felt). Measure drop-off per step. Cut steps until the aha is step two. Two questions: does the page convince me? does the signup talk me out of it? Also check the step *before* the page — the ad or post that set the expectation. Then talk to users on the page, thinking aloud.

## The reset exercise

When a page is chaotic: put it aside, start blank, and allow only the elements that explain what it is and who it's for. Test on 20 strangers. Only then expand.
